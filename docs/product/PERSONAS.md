# Personas

## 1. The Self-Directed Guitarist

This user practices alone and wants structure without feeling trapped inside a course. They move between songs, techniques, theory, and improvisation.

Needs:

- A clear next practice block.
- Repertoire organized by song sections.
- Technique progress tied to BPM and accuracy.
- Fast access to metronome and recordings.
- A diary that remembers what changed.

Risks:

- Too many menu items at once create decision fatigue.
- Dashboard-style metrics feel disconnected from real playing.

## 2. The Multi-Instrument Learner

This user studies guitar, acoustic guitar, keys, and drums, with different goals for each instrument.

Needs:

- Instrument-specific progress.
- Instrument-specific contextual menus.
- Shared music theory where appropriate.
- Separate repertoire, exercises, and practice priorities.
- A visible current instrument in the workspace chrome.

Risks:

- Mixing all instruments in the same view makes the system feel noisy.
- Global progress can hide instrument-specific weaknesses.

## 3. The Technique Builder

This user wants measurable improvement in skills such as alternate picking, bends, legato, rhythm stability, chord changes, or independence.

Needs:

- Skill prerequisites and next requirements.
- Exercises connected to target skills.
- Practice attempts with BPM, accuracy, repetitions, and difficulty.
- Review scheduling and retention cues.
- Evidence of real mastery rather than artificial leveling.

Risks:

- A flat exercise list makes practice feel mechanical.
- Unclear criteria make progress feel arbitrary.

## 4. The Repertoire-Focused Player

This user wants to learn songs deeply, section by section, while understanding which techniques and concepts each song exercises.

Needs:

- Songs with artist, tuning, key, BPM, difficulty, instrument, notes, techniques, scales, and status.
- Sections with independent progress, BPM, notes, tablature, timing ranges, and tone presets.
- Ability to generate a practice plan from a song.
- Recordings attached to song or section context.

Risks:

- Treating songs as simple list items hides the actual study object.
- Song practice becomes detached from skill development.

## 5. The Creative Musician

This user collects riffs, lyrics, references, ideas, versions, and musical sketches.

Needs:

- Projects organized as creative workspaces.
- Riffs and versions visible without opening unrelated study tools.
- Ability to connect ideas to skills, songs, recordings, and future Reaper sessions.
- A lightweight capture path for fragments.

Risks:

- A productivity-app pattern makes creative work feel administrative.
- Over-structured creation can discourage quick capture.

## 6. The Returning User

This user opens Muse Studio after days away and needs to understand what to do without reviewing the entire system.

Needs:

- Continue from last meaningful object.
- Today's plan and remaining time.
- Current objective.
- Recent journal signal.
- Clear paths to resume, review, or explore.

Risks:

- The home view becomes a control panel instead of a re-entry point.
- Too many equal options increase abandonment.
